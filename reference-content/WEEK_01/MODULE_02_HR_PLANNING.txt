1.0  INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

Effective Human Resource Management begins with planning. Before an 
organization can recruit, select, train, compensate, or manage the performance 
of its workforce, it must first answer fundamental questions: How many people 
do we need? What skills and capabilities must they possess? Where will we find 
them? And how will we define the work they will do?

HR Planning and Job Analysis are the twin foundations upon which virtually all 
other HR activities rest. Workforce planning ensures that the organization 
has the right number of people with the right skills in the right positions at 
the right time. Job analysis provides the detailed understanding of work 
content, context, and requirements that informs everything from recruitment 
criteria to compensation levels to performance standards.

This module explores both disciplines in depth, providing you with the 
theoretical knowledge and practical tools needed to conduct effective 
workforce planning and job analysis in any organizational context.

2.0  STRATEGIC WORKFORCE PLANNING
================================================================================

2.1  DEFINING WORKFORCE PLANNING

Workforce planning (also called human resource planning or manpower planning) 
is the systematic process of identifying and addressing the gaps between an 
organization's current workforce and its future workforce needs. It ensures 
that the organization has the human capital necessary to execute its business 
strategy.

The Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development (CIPD) defines 
workforce planning as "a core process of human resource management that is 
shaped by the organizational strategy and ensures the right number of people 
with the right skills are employed in the right place at the right time to 
deliver short- and long-term organizational objectives."

2.2  THE STRATEGIC IMPORTANCE OF WORKFORCE PLANNING

Workforce planning is strategic because it:

a)  LINKS PEOPLE TO STRATEGY: It translates business strategy into talent 
    requirements, ensuring that the organization's human capital is aligned 
    with its strategic direction.

b)  ANTICIPATES CHANGE: It enables organizations to prepare for future 
    workforce needs rather than reacting to shortages or surpluses after 
    they occur.

c)  MANAGES RISK: It identifies critical roles, single points of failure, 
    and skills gaps that could jeopardize business continuity.

d)  OPTIMIZES COSTS: It prevents both the waste of overstaffing and the 
    disruption of understaffing.

e)  ENABLES AGILITY: It creates the workforce flexibility needed to respond 
    to market changes, technology disruptions, and competitive pressures.

2.3  THE WORKFORCE PLANNING PROCESS

A comprehensive workforce planning process typically follows these steps:

STEP 1: UNDERSTAND STRATEGIC DIRECTION
    - Review the organization's mission, vision, values, and strategic plan.
    - Identify strategic priorities, growth plans, new markets, products, 
      or services.
    - Understand the external environment: economic conditions, labor market 
      trends, regulatory changes, technology disruptions.

STEP 2: ANALYZE THE CURRENT WORKFORCE (SUPPLY ANALYSIS)
    - Conduct a workforce audit: headcount, demographics, skills, 
      competencies, performance levels, engagement scores.
    - Map critical roles and key talent.
    - Analyze workforce diversity and representation.
    - Review historical trends: turnover, retirement, internal mobility.

STEP 3: FORECAST FUTURE WORKFORCE NEEDS (DEMAND ANALYSIS)
    - Determine future staffing requirements based on strategic plans.
    - Consider the impact of technology, automation, and process changes 
      on job roles.
    - Project future skills and competency requirements.
    - Factor in planned growth, contraction, or restructuring.

STEP 4: CONDUCT GAP ANALYSIS
    - Compare current workforce (supply) with future needs (demand).
    - Identify quantitative gaps (headcount shortages or surpluses).
    - Identify qualitative gaps (skills, competencies, or capabilities 
      that are missing or need to be developed).

STEP 5: DEVELOP ACTION PLANS
    - Create strategies to close gaps, which may include:
        • Recruitment and hiring (build externally)
        • Training and development (build internally)
        • Redeployment and internal mobility (move talent)
        • Succession planning (prepare for transitions)
        • Outsourcing or contingent workforce (borrow talent)
        • Restructuring or automation (reshape work)
        • Managed attrition or downsizing (reduce surplus)
    - Set timelines, budgets, and accountabilities.
    - Define metrics to measure progress.

STEP 6: IMPLEMENT AND MONITOR
    - Execute action plans.
    - Track progress against metrics.
    - Adjust plans as business conditions change.
    - Report to leadership on workforce planning outcomes.

2.4  LEVELS OF WORKFORCE PLANNING

Workforce planning can be conducted at multiple levels:

    - STRATEGIC LEVEL: Organization-wide, long-term (3–5 years), linked to 
      business strategy. Focuses on overall workforce composition, 
      capability building, and talent pipeline development.

    - TACTICAL LEVEL: Business unit or function-level, medium-term (1–2 
      years). Focuses on staffing plans, budget allocation, and specific 
      talent initiatives.

    - OPERATIONAL LEVEL: Department or team-level, short-term (0–12 months). 
      Focuses on immediate hiring needs, scheduling, and workload management.

3.0  LABOR DEMAND FORECASTING
================================================================================

Demand forecasting is the process of estimating the number and types of 
employees the organization will need at a future point in time. Several 
methods can be used, ranging from simple qualitative judgments to 
sophisticated quantitative models.

3.1  QUALITATIVE METHODS

a)  MANAGERIAL JUDGMENT (Bottom-Up Estimates)
    Managers in each department estimate their future staffing needs based 
    on planned activities, projects, and anticipated workload changes. These 
    estimates are then aggregated upward to produce an overall organizational 
    forecast.

    Advantages: Leverages managers' intimate knowledge of their work.
    Limitations: Subject to bias, inconsistency, and empire-building.

b)  DELPHI TECHNIQUE
    A structured process in which a panel of experts (internal and external) 
    independently provide forecasts, which are then summarized and shared 
    anonymously. The panel iterates through several rounds until consensus 
    is reached.

    Advantages: Reduces groupthink; incorporates diverse perspectives.
    Limitations: Time-consuming; depends on expert quality.

c)  NOMINAL GROUP TECHNIQUE
    Similar to Delphi, but involves face-to-face group discussion. 
    Participants independently generate ideas, then discuss and rank them 
    collectively.

    Advantages: Structured but interactive.
    Limitations: May be influenced by group dynamics.

d)  SCENARIO PLANNING
    The organization develops multiple future scenarios (e.g., high growth, 
    moderate growth, recession) and determines workforce requirements for 
    each scenario. This approach acknowledges uncertainty and helps the 
    organization prepare for different possible futures.

    Advantages: Accounts for uncertainty; builds strategic flexibility.
    Limitations: Requires significant analytical effort.

3.2  QUANTITATIVE METHODS

a)  TREND ANALYSIS
    Uses historical data to project future workforce needs. For example, if 
    a company has historically needed one HR professional for every 100 
    employees, and it projects growing to 5,000 employees, it will need 
    approximately 50 HR professionals.

    Advantages: Simple and easy to apply.
    Limitations: Assumes past trends will continue; ignores structural 
    changes.

b)  RATIO ANALYSIS
    Examines the relationship between a business factor (e.g., sales volume, 
    production output, number of clients) and the number of employees 
    required. For example, if a call center handles 200 calls per agent per 
    day and projects 10,000 daily calls, it needs 50 agents.

    Advantages: Directly ties staffing to business activity.
    Limitations: Assumes stable productivity ratios.

c)  REGRESSION ANALYSIS
    Uses statistical regression to model the relationship between workforce 
    size and one or more business variables (revenue, output, number of 
    locations). The resulting equation can be used to forecast future 
    staffing needs based on projected values of the business variables.

    Advantages: More sophisticated; can incorporate multiple factors.
    Limitations: Requires historical data and statistical expertise; 
    assumes relationships will remain stable.

d)  WORKLOAD ANALYSIS
    Calculates staffing needs based on the volume of work to be performed, 
    the time required for each unit of work, and the available working hours. 
    For example:

    Number of employees needed = (Total workload in hours) / 
                                 (Available hours per employee per period)

    Advantages: Precise and activity-based.
    Limitations: Requires detailed work measurement data.

e)  COMPUTER SIMULATION AND MODELING
    Advanced techniques use mathematical models and computer simulations to 
    project workforce flows (hiring, promotions, transfers, separations) 
    over time under different assumptions. Markov chain analysis is one 
    commonly used technique that models transition probabilities between 
    workforce states (e.g., the probability of an employee being promoted, 
    transferred, or leaving in a given year).

    Advantages: Sophisticated; can model complex scenarios.
    Limitations: Requires significant data and technical expertise.

4.0  LABOR SUPPLY ANALYSIS
================================================================================

Supply analysis examines the availability of workers to meet forecasted 
demand. It considers both internal supply (current employees who can be 
developed, promoted, or redeployed) and external supply (candidates available 
in the labor market).

4.1  INTERNAL SUPPLY ANALYSIS

a)  SKILLS INVENTORIES
    A skills inventory is a database of information about current employees, 
    including their education, training, certifications, skills, languages, 
    work experience, performance ratings, career preferences, and 
    development potential. This data allows planners to assess what talent 
    is already available within the organization.

b)  REPLACEMENT CHARTS
    Replacement charts are visual representations that show key positions 
    in the organization and identify potential successors for each position, 
    along with their readiness level (ready now, ready in 1–2 years, or 
    needs development).

c)  TRANSITION MATRICES (MARKOV ANALYSIS)
    A transition matrix shows the historical patterns of employee movement 
    within the organization. For each job category, it shows the probability 
    that an employee will:
        - Remain in the same position
        - Be promoted to a higher position
        - Transfer to a different position
        - Leave the organization (voluntary or involuntary)

    By applying these probabilities to the current workforce, planners can 
    project the future internal supply of employees in each job category.

    Example Transition Matrix:
    
    From / To        Entry   Mid    Senior  Exit
    Entry Level      0.60    0.15   0.00    0.25
    Mid Level        0.00    0.65   0.20    0.15
    Senior Level     0.00    0.00   0.80    0.20

    This matrix tells us that, historically, 60% of entry-level employees 
    remain at the entry level, 15% are promoted to mid-level, and 25% 
    leave the organization.

d)  TURNOVER ANALYSIS
    Analyzing historical turnover patterns—by department, tenure, 
    performance level, age group, and other factors—helps predict how many 
    employees are likely to leave the organization. Key metrics include:

    - Overall turnover rate = (Number of separations / Average headcount) 
      × 100
    - Voluntary turnover rate (resignations only)
    - Involuntary turnover rate (terminations, layoffs)
    - First-year turnover rate (new hires who leave within 12 months)
    - Regretted turnover rate (high performers who leave voluntarily)

4.2  EXTERNAL SUPPLY ANALYSIS

External supply analysis examines the availability of qualified candidates 
in the labor market. Factors to consider include:

    - LABOR MARKET CONDITIONS: Unemployment rates, labor force participation 
      rates, and industry-specific labor availability.
    - DEMOGRAPHIC TRENDS: Aging workforce, generational shifts, immigration 
      patterns, population growth or decline in key regions.
    - EDUCATIONAL PIPELINE: The number of graduates entering the labor 
      market with relevant qualifications.
    - COMPETITOR ACTIVITY: Hiring patterns, compensation levels, and 
      employer branding of competing organizations.
    - GEOGRAPHIC FACTORS: Location-specific labor availability, commuting 
      patterns, and willingness to relocate.
    - REGULATORY FACTORS: Immigration policies, licensing requirements, 
      and occupational regulations.

5.0  BALANCING SUPPLY AND DEMAND
================================================================================

Once demand and supply have been forecasted, the gap analysis reveals one 
of three possible outcomes:

5.1  LABOR SHORTAGE (Demand > Supply)
    When the organization needs more employees than are projected to be 
    available. Strategies include:
        - Accelerate recruitment and hiring
        - Expand sourcing channels (new geographies, demographics)
        - Develop internal talent through training and upskilling
        - Use contingent workers (temps, contractors, freelancers)
        - Offer premium compensation to attract scarce talent
        - Redesign work or automate tasks to reduce labor requirements
        - Implement retention strategies to reduce turnover
        - Partner with educational institutions for pipeline development

5.2  LABOR SURPLUS (Supply > Demand)
    When the organization has more employees than it needs. Strategies 
    include:
        - Hiring freeze (stop external recruitment)
        - Managed attrition (do not replace departing employees)
        - Voluntary early retirement programs
        - Retraining and redeployment to other roles or locations
        - Reduced work hours or job sharing
        - Layoffs and redundancies (as a last resort)
        - Outplacement services and transition support

5.3  SKILLS MISMATCH (Right Number, Wrong Skills)
    When the headcount is adequate but the skills mix does not match future 
    requirements. Strategies include:
        - Reskilling and upskilling programs
        - Internal mobility and job rotation
        - Targeted external recruitment for critical skill gaps
        - Strategic outsourcing for specialized capabilities
        - Partnerships with technology providers or consultants

6.0  SUCCESSION PLANNING
================================================================================

6.1  DEFINITION AND PURPOSE

Succession planning is the systematic process of identifying and developing 
internal candidates to fill key leadership and critical positions in the 
organization. It ensures business continuity by preparing the organization 
for planned and unplanned vacancies in roles that are essential to 
organizational success.

6.2  KEY ELEMENTS OF SUCCESSION PLANNING

a)  IDENTIFYING CRITICAL POSITIONS: Not all positions require succession 
    plans. Focus on roles that:
        - Are essential to business operations or strategy execution
        - Would cause significant disruption if vacated unexpectedly
        - Require specialized knowledge or long development timelines
        - Are at high risk of vacancy (incumbents nearing retirement, etc.)

b)  ASSESSING TALENT: Evaluate potential successors on:
        - Current performance (how well they perform in their current role)
        - Future potential (their ability to grow into larger roles)
        - Readiness (how soon they could step into the target role)
        - Flight risk (likelihood of leaving the organization)

c)  DEVELOPING SUCCESSORS: Create individualized development plans that may 
    include:
        - Stretch assignments and special projects
        - Cross-functional rotations
        - Mentoring and coaching relationships
        - Formal leadership development programs
        - External education (MBA, executive education)
        - Acting or interim assignments in the target role

d)  REVIEWING AND UPDATING: Succession plans should be reviewed at least 
    annually, with input from senior leadership. The review should assess:
        - Changes in business strategy that affect critical roles
        - Progress of successor development
        - New talent entering the pipeline
        - Departures or changes in readiness assessments

6.3  THE 9-BOX GRID

A widely used tool in succession planning is the 9-Box Grid, which plots 
employees on two dimensions:

    - Performance (low, moderate, high) on the X-axis
    - Potential (low, moderate, high) on the Y-axis

This produces nine categories:

                    LOW POTENTIAL    MOD POTENTIAL    HIGH POTENTIAL
    HIGH PERF       Solid Perf      Key Player       Star / HiPo
    MOD PERF        Average         Core Player      Growth Employee
    LOW PERF        Risk / Exit     Underperformer   Enigma

Each category implies a different management approach:
    - Stars/HiPos: Fast-track development, retention investment
    - Key Players: Maintain engagement, provide growth opportunities
    - Core Players: Develop incrementally, ensure recognition
    - Underperformers: Performance improvement plans
    - Risk/Exit: Manage out or redeploy

7.0  JOB ANALYSIS: FOUNDATIONS
================================================================================

7.1  DEFINING JOB ANALYSIS

Job analysis is the systematic process of collecting, documenting, and 
analyzing information about the content, context, and requirements of a 
job. It answers two fundamental questions:

    1. WHAT does the job involve? (Tasks, duties, responsibilities, and 
       working conditions)
    2. WHAT does the job require? (Knowledge, skills, abilities, and other 
       characteristics—KSAOs—needed to perform the job successfully)

7.2  TERMINOLOGY

Understanding key terminology is essential:

    - TASK: A distinct, identifiable work activity (e.g., "review customer 
      applications for completeness").
    - DUTY: A collection of related tasks (e.g., "process customer 
      applications").
    - POSITION: The set of tasks and duties performed by one person (e.g., 
      "Application Processor, Position #A-1234").
    - JOB: A group of similar positions (e.g., "Application Processor"—
      there may be 20 people in this job).
    - JOB FAMILY: A group of related jobs (e.g., "Customer Service" might 
      include Application Processors, Customer Service Representatives, 
      and Claims Reviewers).
    - OCCUPATION: A broad grouping of similar jobs across organizations 
      (e.g., "Customer Service" as classified in O*NET or the Bureau of 
      Labor Statistics).

7.3  PURPOSES OF JOB ANALYSIS

Job analysis serves as the foundation for numerous HR activities:

    - RECRUITMENT: Defines what to look for in candidates.
    - SELECTION: Informs the design of interviews, tests, and assessment 
      criteria.
    - COMPENSATION: Provides the basis for job evaluation and pay grading.
    - PERFORMANCE MANAGEMENT: Establishes performance standards and 
      evaluation criteria.
    - TRAINING: Identifies the skills and knowledge that employees need 
      to develop.
    - CAREER DEVELOPMENT: Maps career paths and progression requirements.
    - COMPLIANCE: Supports the legal defensibility of HR decisions 
      (especially in cases of alleged discrimination).
    - WORKFORCE PLANNING: Informs the categorization and analysis of jobs 
      and roles.
    - HEALTH AND SAFETY: Identifies job hazards and physical requirements.

7.4  LEGAL IMPORTANCE OF JOB ANALYSIS

Job analysis plays a critical role in legal compliance. Under employment 
discrimination laws, selection criteria must be job-related and consistent 
with business necessity. A thorough job analysis provides the documentation 
needed to demonstrate that:
    - Selection criteria (tests, interview questions, qualifications) are 
      based on actual job requirements, not arbitrary or discriminatory 
      standards.
    - Essential functions of the job have been identified (important under 
      the Americans with Disabilities Act for reasonable accommodation 
      determinations).
    - Performance standards are based on actual job duties and expectations.

8.0  JOB ANALYSIS METHODS
================================================================================

Multiple methods can be used to collect job analysis data. The choice of 
method depends on the type of job, available resources, and intended use 
of the results.

8.1  OBSERVATION

The analyst directly observes employees performing the job and records 
what they do, how they do it, and the conditions under which they work.

    Best suited for: Jobs with observable, physical, and repetitive tasks 
    (e.g., assembly line, retail, skilled trades).
    
    Advantages: Direct, firsthand data; reveals actual behaviors.
    Limitations: Not suitable for mental or knowledge-intensive work; 
    time-consuming; observer may influence behavior (Hawthorne Effect).

8.2  INTERVIEWS

The analyst interviews job incumbents, supervisors, and/or subject matter 
experts to gather information about the job. Interviews may be structured 
(using a standardized set of questions) or unstructured (open-ended 
discussion).

    Best suited for: Any type of job, especially complex or managerial roles.
    
    Advantages: Rich, detailed information; allows follow-up questions; 
    captures context and nuance.
    Limitations: Time-consuming; subject to bias and selective reporting; 
    interviewees may exaggerate or omit duties.

8.3  QUESTIONNAIRES AND SURVEYS

Job incumbents and supervisors complete standardized questionnaires about 
job tasks, responsibilities, skills, and working conditions. This approach 
can efficiently gather data from many employees simultaneously.

    Best suited for: Large-scale analysis of multiple jobs; jobs performed 
    by many employees.
    
    Advantages: Efficient; can cover many jobs quickly; produces 
    quantifiable data.
    Limitations: May miss important details; response quality varies; 
    requires careful questionnaire design.

8.4  POSITION ANALYSIS QUESTIONNAIRE (PAQ)

Developed by Ernest McCormick, the PAQ is a structured job analysis 
instrument containing 194 items organized into six divisions:

    1. Information Input: Where and how the worker gets information
    2. Mental Processes: Reasoning, decision-making, planning
    3. Work Output: Physical activities and tools used
    4. Relationships with Others: Communication and interpersonal 
       interactions
    5. Job Context: Physical and social working conditions
    6. Other Characteristics: Schedule, responsibilities, requirements

    Advantages: Standardized; quantitative; allows comparison across jobs; 
    well-researched.
    Limitations: Complex; requires trained analysts; may not capture 
    job-specific nuances; reading level can be challenging.

8.5  FUNCTIONAL JOB ANALYSIS (FJA)

Developed by Sidney Fine, FJA describes what workers do in terms of their 
interactions with three categories:

    - DATA: Information, ideas, statistics (e.g., compiling, analyzing, 
      coordinating)
    - PEOPLE: Clients, coworkers, supervisors (e.g., mentoring, 
      negotiating, supervising)
    - THINGS: Machines, tools, equipment (e.g., handling, operating, 
      precision working)

Each function is rated on a complexity scale, with higher ratings indicating 
more complex involvement.

    Advantages: Systematic; provides a common language for describing jobs.
    Limitations: Can be abstract; may not capture all relevant job aspects.

8.6  CRITICAL INCIDENT TECHNIQUE (CIT)

Job incumbents and supervisors describe specific incidents of particularly 
effective or ineffective job performance. These incidents reveal the 
behaviors, skills, and decisions that distinguish excellent performance 
from poor performance.

    Advantages: Focuses on actual behavior; identifies key success factors; 
    useful for developing behavioral interview questions and BARS scales.
    Limitations: Focuses on extremes; may miss routine tasks; 
    time-consuming to collect and analyze.

8.7  WORK DIARIES AND LOGS

Job incumbents record their activities throughout the workday over a 
specified period (typically 1–4 weeks). The diary captures what they do, 
how long it takes, and any relevant context.

    Advantages: Captures real-time data; covers the full range of tasks.
    Limitations: Time-consuming for incumbents; compliance varies; may 
    alter normal behavior.

8.8  O*NET (OCCUPATIONAL INFORMATION NETWORK)

Maintained by the U.S. Department of Labor, O*NET is a comprehensive online 
database containing detailed information about hundreds of occupations. For 
each occupation, O*NET provides data on:

    - Tasks and work activities
    - Knowledge, skills, and abilities
    - Work styles and values
    - Education and experience requirements
    - Wage and employment outlook

    Advantages: Free; comprehensive; regularly updated; covers all major 
    occupations.
    Limitations: Generic (not organization-specific); U.S.-focused.

9.0  JOB DESCRIPTIONS
================================================================================

9.1  WHAT IS A JOB DESCRIPTION?

A job description is a written document that summarizes the key information 
derived from a job analysis. It describes what the job involves and serves 
as the foundation for many HR activities, including recruitment, selection, 
performance management, and compensation.

9.2  COMPONENTS OF A JOB DESCRIPTION

A well-written job description typically includes:

a)  JOB TITLE: A clear, standardized title that accurately reflects the 
    job's level and function (e.g., "Senior Financial Analyst" rather than 
    "Finance Ninja").

b)  JOB SUMMARY / PURPOSE: A brief (2–3 sentence) overview of the job's 
    primary purpose and how it fits into the organization.

c)  REPORTING RELATIONSHIPS: Who the job reports to (supervisor/manager) 
    and who reports to it (direct reports, if any).

d)  KEY DUTIES AND RESPONSIBILITIES: A list of the major duties and 
    responsibilities, typically 8–12 items, each beginning with an action 
    verb. Listed in order of importance or frequency.

e)  ESSENTIAL FUNCTIONS: Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), 
    essential functions are the fundamental duties that the employee must 
    be able to perform, with or without reasonable accommodation.

f)  QUALIFICATIONS:
    - Education requirements
    - Experience requirements
    - Required certifications or licenses
    - Required skills and competencies

g)  PHYSICAL REQUIREMENTS: Any physical demands of the job (lifting, 
    standing, travel, etc.), important for ADA compliance.

h)  WORKING CONDITIONS: Work environment, schedule, travel requirements, 
    and any unusual conditions (noise, temperature, hazardous materials).

i)  SALARY RANGE / GRADE: Many organizations include the pay grade or 
    salary range for transparency.

j)  DISCLAIMER: A standard statement noting that the job description is 
    not exhaustive and may be modified.

9.3  BEST PRACTICES FOR WRITING JOB DESCRIPTIONS

    - Use clear, concise language. Avoid jargon and acronyms.
    - Begin each duty statement with an action verb (e.g., "Develop," 
      "Manage," "Analyze," "Coordinate").
    - Focus on outcomes and results, not just activities.
    - Distinguish between essential and non-essential functions.
    - Be specific about requirements; avoid vague terms like "some 
      experience."
    - Review and update regularly (at least annually).
    - Ensure compliance with anti-discrimination laws (avoid requirements 
      that could be discriminatory unless they are genuinely job-related).
    - Get input from job incumbents and supervisors to ensure accuracy.
    - Use gender-neutral language.

9.4  SAMPLE JOB DESCRIPTION

    JOB TITLE: Human Resources Coordinator
    DEPARTMENT: Human Resources
    REPORTS TO: HR Manager
    FLSA STATUS: Non-Exempt
    LAST UPDATED: January 2024

    JOB SUMMARY:
    The Human Resources Coordinator provides administrative support to the 
    HR department in areas including recruitment, employee records, benefits 
    administration, and compliance. This role serves as the first point of 
    contact for employee HR inquiries.

    KEY DUTIES AND RESPONSIBILITIES:
    1. Coordinate the recruitment process, including posting job openings, 
       screening resumes, scheduling interviews, and communicating with 
       candidates. (25%)
    2. Maintain accurate and up-to-date employee records in the HRIS, 
       ensuring data integrity and confidentiality. (20%)
    3. Administer employee benefits enrollment, changes, and inquiries, 
       liaising with benefits providers as needed. (15%)
    4. Assist with new employee onboarding, including orientation 
       scheduling, document collection, and system access setup. (10%)
    5. Prepare and process HR documents, including offer letters, 
       employment verifications, and separation paperwork. (10%)
    6. Respond to employee inquiries regarding HR policies, procedures, 
       and programs. (10%)
    7. Support compliance activities, including EEO reporting, I-9 
       audits, and policy updates. (5%)
    8. Assist with planning and coordination of employee events, 
       recognition programs, and training sessions. (5%)

    QUALIFICATIONS:
    Required:
    - Bachelor's degree in Human Resources, Business Administration, or 
      related field.
    - 1–2 years of HR or administrative experience.
    - Proficiency in Microsoft Office Suite and HRIS systems.
    - Strong organizational skills and attention to detail.
    - Excellent written and verbal communication skills.
    - Ability to handle sensitive and confidential information with 
      discretion.

    Preferred:
    - SHRM-CP certification or PHR certification.
    - Experience with ADP, Workday, or similar HRIS platforms.
    - Knowledge of employment law fundamentals.

    PHYSICAL REQUIREMENTS:
    - Primarily sedentary work in an office environment.
    - Occasional lifting up to 20 pounds (files, event supplies).

    WORKING CONDITIONS:
    - Standard office hours: Monday–Friday, 8:30 AM – 5:00 PM.
    - Occasional overtime during peak periods (open enrollment, year-end).
    - Hybrid work arrangement (3 days in-office, 2 days remote).

10.0  PERSON SPECIFICATIONS AND COMPETENCY PROFILES
================================================================================

10.1  PERSON SPECIFICATION

While a job description describes the job, a person specification describes 
the ideal person to fill the job. It translates job requirements into 
human attributes, specifying the knowledge, skills, abilities, and other 
characteristics (KSAOs) needed for successful job performance.

Components of a person specification:

a)  KNOWLEDGE: The body of information needed for the job.
    Example: Knowledge of employment law; knowledge of accounting 
    principles.

b)  SKILLS: Proficiency in performing specific tasks.
    Example: Skill in using HRIS software; skill in conducting interviews.

c)  ABILITIES: Innate capacities or aptitudes.
    Example: Ability to analyze complex data; ability to communicate 
    clearly in writing.

d)  OTHER CHARACTERISTICS: Personal attributes, certifications, physical 
    requirements, or other factors.
    Example: Professional certification (CPA, SHRM-CP); willingness to 
    travel; valid driver's license.

For each KSAO, specify whether it is:
    - ESSENTIAL (must have for the job) or DESIRABLE (nice to have but 
      not required)
    - WHERE IT WILL BE ASSESSED: Application form, interview, test, 
      references, etc.

10.2  COMPETENCY PROFILES

Competencies are broader than KSAOs and describe the observable behaviors 
and underlying characteristics that distinguish superior performers from 
average performers in a given role. A competency typically includes:

    - COMPETENCY NAME (e.g., "Strategic Thinking")
    - DEFINITION (e.g., "The ability to analyze complex situations, 
      anticipate future trends, and develop long-term plans aligned with 
      organizational goals.")
    - BEHAVIORAL INDICATORS (e.g., "Identifies patterns and trends in 
      data to inform decision-making; considers the long-term implications 
      of decisions; aligns team activities with organizational strategy.")
    - PROFICIENCY LEVELS (e.g., Basic, Proficient, Advanced, Expert)

Competency profiles describe the set of competencies required for a 
particular role or job family. They are used for:
    - Selection (behavioral interview questions)
    - Performance management (competency-based evaluation)
    - Development planning (competency gap analysis)
    - Career pathing (progression from one competency level to the next)
    - Succession planning (assessing readiness for target roles)

10.3  EXAMPLE COMPETENCY: "STAKEHOLDER MANAGEMENT"

    Name: Stakeholder Management
    
    Definition: The ability to identify, engage, and influence key 
    stakeholders to build productive working relationships and achieve 
    mutually beneficial outcomes.
    
    Behavioral Indicators:
    Level 1 (Basic): Identifies key stakeholders for their area of work. 
    Responds to stakeholder inquiries in a timely manner. Communicates 
    clearly and professionally.
    
    Level 2 (Proficient): Proactively builds relationships with 
    stakeholders. Understands stakeholder needs and concerns. Tailors 
    communication style to different audiences. Manages stakeholder 
    expectations effectively.
    
    Level 3 (Advanced): Influences senior stakeholders to gain support 
    for initiatives. Navigates complex stakeholder dynamics. Resolves 
    conflicts between stakeholders. Builds strategic alliances across 
    the organization.
    
    Level 4 (Expert): Shapes the organization's approach to stakeholder 
    engagement. Represents the organization externally with senior 
    external stakeholders. Mentors others in stakeholder management.

11.0  THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN JOB ANALYSIS AND OTHER HR FUNCTIONS
================================================================================

Job analysis is not a standalone activity—it is the foundation upon which 
many other HR functions depend. The following diagram illustrates these 
relationships:

                           JOB ANALYSIS
                               |
            +---------+--------+--------+---------+
            |         |        |        |         |
        Recruitment  Comp-   Training  Perf     Health
        & Selection  ensation  & Dev    Mgmt    & Safety
            |         |        |        |         |
        Job criteria  Job     Skills   Perf     Physical
        & interview  evalua-  gap     stand-   require-
        questions    tion &   identi-  ards &   ments &
                     grading  fication KPIs     hazards

Specifically:

    - RECRUITMENT & SELECTION: Job analysis defines the criteria used to 
      screen candidates, design interview questions, select assessment 
      methods, and evaluate applicants.

    - COMPENSATION: Job evaluation (which determines pay grades) requires 
      detailed job analysis data to assess the relative value of jobs based 
      on factors like skill, effort, responsibility, and working conditions.

    - TRAINING & DEVELOPMENT: Comparing job requirements (from job analysis) 
      with employee capabilities reveals training needs and development 
      priorities.

    - PERFORMANCE MANAGEMENT: Job analysis provides the standards and 
      criteria against which employee performance is evaluated.

    - HEALTH & SAFETY: Job analysis identifies physical requirements, 
      hazards, and working conditions that inform safety programs and 
      accommodation decisions.

    - WORKFORCE PLANNING: Job analysis provides the job categories and 
      classifications used in workforce planning models.

    - LEGAL COMPLIANCE: Job analysis documentation supports the legal 
      defensibility of HR practices by demonstrating that decisions are 
      based on legitimate, job-related criteria.

12.0  SUMMARY AND KEY TAKEAWAYS
================================================================================

1.  Strategic workforce planning is the systematic process of ensuring the 
    organization has the right people with the right skills in the right 
    positions at the right time. It links human capital to business strategy.

2.  Demand forecasting uses qualitative methods (managerial judgment, Delphi 
    technique, scenario planning) and quantitative methods (trend analysis, 
    ratio analysis, regression, workload analysis) to project future 
    staffing needs.

3.  Supply analysis examines both internal sources (skills inventories, 
    replacement charts, transition matrices) and external sources (labor 
    market conditions, demographics, educational pipelines).

4.  Gap analysis reveals shortages, surpluses, or skills mismatches, each 
    requiring different strategic responses.

5.  Succession planning identifies and develops internal talent for critical 
    leadership positions, ensuring organizational continuity.

6.  Job analysis is the systematic study of job content and requirements, 
    providing the foundation for recruitment, compensation, performance 
    management, training, and compliance.

7.  Multiple job analysis methods exist, each with strengths and 
    limitations. Effective practice often combines multiple methods for a 
    comprehensive understanding.

8.  Job descriptions and person specifications translate job analysis data 
    into practical documents that guide HR activities.

9.  Competency profiles provide a behavioral framework for selection, 
    development, and performance management.
