TOPIC 1: UNDERSTANDING HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT (HRM)
1.1 What is Human Resource Management?
Human Resource Management (HRM) is the process of managing people within an organisation to achieve organisational objectives while ensuring employee satisfaction, development and well-being.

In simple terms, HRM involves managing the entire working relationship between an organisation and its employees.

Every organisation needs people to perform different responsibilities.

For example, consider a hospital:
Department	Employees Required
Medical Department	Doctors
Nursing Department	Nurses
Laboratory	Lab Technicians
Biomedical Department	Biomedical Engineers
Administration	Administrative Staff
Finance	Accountants
Human Resources	HR Executives


The HR department ensures that the organisation has the right people, in the right positions, with the right skills, at the right time.

However, HR's responsibility does not end after hiring someone.

It continues throughout the employee's association with the organisation, from recruitment and onboarding to performance management, employee development and separation.
1.2 Understanding the Term Human Resources
The term Human Resources consists of two words:

Human: The individuals who contribute their knowledge, skills, abilities, experience and efforts to an organisation.

Resources: The people, materials, finances and other assets an organisation uses to achieve its objectives.

Human resources refer to the employees who contribute to an organisation through their abilities and work.

Unlike machines or financial resources, employees have individual expectations, emotions, motivations, ambitions and development needs.

This makes managing human resources a specialised organisational responsibility.
1.3 Definition of HRM
For academic and professional understanding:

Human Resource Management is a systematic approach to acquiring, developing, motivating, compensating and retaining employees while aligning their individual contributions with organisational goals.

Remember these five important words:
- Acquiring – Hiring suitable employees.
- Developing – Improving employee knowledge and skills.
- Motivating – Encouraging employees to perform effectively.
- Compensating – Providing salaries, benefits and rewards.
- Retaining – Encouraging valuable employees to remain with the organisation.
These five concepts form the foundation of HRM.
TOPIC 2: OBJECTIVES OF HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT
HRM has several objectives. They can broadly be classified into four categories.
2.1 Organisational Objectives
The primary objective of HRM is to help an organisation achieve its goals by managing its workforce effectively.

Examples:
- Recruiting qualified employees.
- Improving employee productivity.
- Reducing unnecessary employee turnover.
- Ensuring appropriate staffing levels.
- Improving organisational performance.
Practical example:

A hospital is experiencing a shortage of nurses.

The HR department identifies the staffing requirement, prepares job descriptions, recruits qualified nurses and coordinates their onboarding.

This helps the hospital maintain its operations and patient-care services.
2.2 Functional Objectives
These objectives ensure that the HR department itself operates efficiently.

Examples:
- Maintaining accurate employee records.
- Processing recruitment activities on time.
- Managing payroll coordination.
- Handling employee grievances.
- Maintaining HR policies and procedures.
Practical example:

If the hospital has 100 employees, HR must maintain their employee records, attendance information, joining documents and relevant employment information.
2.3 Personal Objectives
Employees have individual expectations and professional goals.

HRM attempts to support these objectives while balancing organisational requirements.

Examples:
- Fair compensation.
- Career growth opportunities.
- Training and development.
- Employee recognition.
- Work-life balance.
- Safe working conditions.
Practical example:

A Biomedical Engineer wants to develop expertise in advanced medical equipment.

HR can coordinate with the department head to identify suitable technical training opportunities.
2.4 Societal Objectives
Organisations also have responsibilities towards society.

HRM helps organisations maintain ethical and socially responsible employment practices.

Examples:
- Equal employment opportunities.
- Prevention of workplace harassment.
- Compliance with applicable labour laws.
- Employee health and safety.
- Non-discriminatory recruitment.
- Ethical workplace practices.
Practical example:

A company must ensure that recruitment decisions are based on relevant qualifications and job requirements rather than unfair discrimination.
Summary
Objective	Main Focus
Organisational	Achieving company goals
Functional	Efficient HR department operations
Personal	Employee needs and development
Societal	Ethical and socially responsible practices


Important: These four objectives are frequently used to explain the purpose of HRM in academic discussions and interviews.
TOPIC 3: IMPORTANCE OF HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT
Why does an organisation need an HR department?

An organisation may have excellent infrastructure, technology and financial resources. However, without capable employees, it cannot operate effectively.

HRM is important for the following reasons.
3.1 Recruitment of Suitable Employees
HR ensures that vacancies are filled with individuals who possess the required qualifications, knowledge and skills.

For example, a hospital requires a qualified Lab Technician.

HR coordinates the recruitment process, screens applications and arranges interviews with the concerned department.
3.2 Employee Training and Development
Employees require continuous learning to improve their performance.

HR identifies training requirements and coordinates suitable development programmes.

Examples:
- Communication training.
- Leadership development.
- Workplace safety training.
- Technical training.
- Software training.
3.3 Employee Motivation
Motivated employees are generally more engaged and productive.

HR supports employee motivation through:
- Recognition programmes.
- Rewards and incentives.
- Career development opportunities.
- Employee engagement initiatives.
- Fair compensation practices.
3.4 Employee Retention
Employee retention refers to an organisation's ability to retain its employees over a period of time.

High employee turnover can increase recruitment expenses, training costs and operational disruption.

HR works towards identifying reasons for employee dissatisfaction and developing appropriate retention initiatives.
3.5 Maintaining Workplace Relationships
HR acts as an important link between employees and management.

It helps address:
- Workplace conflicts.
- Employee complaints.
- Communication gaps.
- Grievances.
- Disciplinary matters.
HR must remain professional, impartial and follow appropriate organisational procedures.
3.6 Legal and Regulatory Compliance
Organisations must follow applicable employment-related laws and regulations.

HR helps maintain compliance through:
- Employee documentation.
- Payroll-related records.
- Applicable social security requirements.
- Workplace safety procedures.
- POSH compliance.
- Statutory records and reporting.
3.7 Performance Improvement
HR helps organisations establish performance evaluation systems.

These systems help identify:
- High-performing employees.
- Employees requiring additional support.
- Training requirements.
- Performance gaps.
- Development opportunities.
3.8 Organisational Culture
Organisational culture refers to the shared values, beliefs, behaviours and practices that influence how people work within an organisation.

HR contributes to creating a workplace that encourages:
- Professionalism.
- Collaboration.
- Accountability.
- Respect.
- Ethical behaviour.
- Inclusion.
TOPIC 4: FUNCTIONS OF HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT
This is one of the most important topics in today's lesson.

HR functions can broadly be divided into two categories:
1. Managerial Functions.
2. Operative Functions.
4.1 Managerial Functions
Managerial functions involve planning, organising, directing and controlling HR activities.
A. Planning
HR planning involves determining the organisation's future workforce requirements.

It answers questions such as:
- How many employees are required?
- Which departments need additional employees?
- What skills are needed?
- When should recruitment begin?
- What is the expected workforce budget?
Example:

A hospital plans to open a new department.

HR estimates the number of doctors, nurses, technicians and administrative employees required.
B. Organising
Organising involves arranging HR activities, responsibilities and resources systematically.

It includes:
- Defining employee responsibilities.
- Establishing reporting structures.
- Allocating HR duties.
- Creating departmental coordination processes.
Example:

The HR Manager assigns one HR Executive to recruitment and another to employee documentation and attendance coordination.
C. Directing
Directing involves guiding, motivating and supervising employees to achieve organisational objectives.

It includes:
- Leadership.
- Communication.
- Motivation.
- Guidance.
- Supervision.
Example:

An HR Manager guides the recruitment team to complete hiring requirements before a specified deadline.
D. Controlling
Controlling involves monitoring HR activities and comparing actual performance with planned objectives.

It includes:
- Monitoring recruitment targets.
- Checking payroll accuracy.
- Reviewing employee attendance.
- Evaluating training effectiveness.
- Identifying deviations and corrective actions.
Example:

If the recruitment target is to hire 10 employees within 30 days but only four have joined, HR investigates the reasons and takes corrective action.
4.2 Operative Functions
Operative functions are the actual activities performed by HR to manage employees.

These are particularly important for someone entering HR Operations or HR Generalist roles.
A. Procurement Function
Procurement refers to obtaining the required workforce for an organisation.

It includes:
1. Human Resource Planning.
2. Job Analysis.
3. Recruitment.
4. Selection.
5. Placement.
Example:

A hospital needs three nurses.

HR identifies the requirement, prepares the job description, sources candidates, conducts screening and coordinates selection.
B. Development Function
Development focuses on improving employee knowledge, abilities and professional capabilities.

It includes:
- Employee training.
- Skill development.
- Career planning.
- Leadership development.
- Performance improvement.
Example:

A hospital introduces new medical equipment.

HR coordinates training so that relevant employees understand its correct operation and safety requirements.
C. Compensation Function
Compensation refers to the financial and non-financial rewards provided to employees in exchange for their work.

It includes:
- Basic salary.
- Allowances.
- Incentives.
- Bonuses.
- Benefits.
- Recognition and rewards.
HR typically coordinates compensation policies and payroll-related activities with finance.
D. Integration Function
Integration focuses on maintaining positive relationships between employees and management.

It includes:
- Employee engagement.
- Grievance handling.
- Conflict resolution.
- Employee communication.
- Workplace discipline.
- Employee relations.
Example:

Two employees from different departments have a disagreement affecting their work.

HR facilitates an appropriate discussion and follows organisational procedures to address the issue.
E. Maintenance Function
Maintenance focuses on preserving employee well-being and maintaining a safe and supportive working environment.

It includes:
- Workplace health and safety.
- Employee welfare.
- Employee benefits.
- Leave administration.
- Employee records.
- Working conditions.
Example:

HR ensures that hospital employees receive relevant workplace safety training and that applicable employee welfare procedures are followed.
F. Separation Function
Separation refers to the end of the employment relationship between an employee and the organisation.

It includes:
- Resignation processing.
- Retirement.
- Termination.
- Exit interviews.
- Knowledge transfer.
- Asset clearance.
- Full and final settlement coordination.
- Issuing relevant employment documents.
Example:

An employee submits a resignation.

HR coordinates the notice period, exit formalities, clearance and relevant documentation.
Managerial vs Operative Functions
Managerial Functions	Operative Functions
Planning	Procurement
Organising	Development
Directing	Compensation
Controlling	Integration
	Maintenance
	Separation


Remember: Managerial functions explain how HR activities are managed. Operative functions explain the specific activities HR performs.
TOPIC 5: EVOLUTION OF HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT
HRM did not develop in its current form overnight.

It evolved as organisations began recognising that employees are not simply workers who perform assigned tasks. They are valuable contributors to organisational success.

Understand the following three stages.
5.1 Personnel Management
Personnel Management represents an earlier approach to managing employees.

Its primary focus was administrative and employment-related activities.

Major responsibilities included:
- Employee record maintenance.
- Attendance management.
- Salary administration.
- Hiring and termination.
- Maintaining workplace discipline.
- Ensuring basic employment compliance.
Example:

A factory's personnel department maintains employee attendance registers, salary records and appointment documents.

The focus is mainly on administration and maintaining employment records.
5.2 Human Resource Management
Human Resource Management introduced a broader approach.

Employees began to be viewed as valuable organisational resources whose performance and development contribute to business success.

Its focus expanded to include:
- Recruitment and selection.
- Employee training.
- Performance management.
- Employee motivation.
- Compensation and benefits.
- Employee engagement.
- Retention.
- Workplace relationships.
Example:

Instead of merely maintaining attendance records, an HR department also analyses absenteeism, identifies underlying problems and develops initiatives to improve attendance.
5.3 Strategic Human Resource Management
Strategic HRM connects workforce management with the organisation's long-term business objectives.

HR becomes involved in organisational planning and decision-making.

Its responsibilities include:
- Workforce forecasting.
- Leadership development.
- Succession planning.
- Organisational development.
- Talent management.
- Workforce productivity.
- Strategic employee retention.
Example:

A hospital plans to expand into three new locations over the next two years.

Strategic HR planning identifies the future requirements for doctors, nurses, technicians and administrative personnel.

It also plans recruitment timelines, training budgets and leadership requirements.
Comparison
Basis	Personnel Management	HRM	Strategic HRM
Main Focus	Administration	Employee management	Business strategy
Employee View	Workforce/labour	Valuable resource	Strategic contributor
Approach	Primarily administrative	Operational and developmental	Long-term and strategic
Training	Limited or task-focused	Employee skill development	Future workforce capabilities
Decision-making	Mainly administrative	HR and management	Integrated with business leadership
Objective	Maintain employment records and processes	Improve workforce effectiveness	Achieve long-term organisational goals


Important Note: These stages are useful conceptual distinctions. In practice, many organisations perform activities associated with all three approaches simultaneously.
TOPIC 6: PRACTICAL UNDERSTANDING OF HRM IN A HOSPITAL
Now connect everything to an actual workplace environment.

Consider a hospital with 100 employees.

The hospital has the following requirements:
1. Five new nurses need to be recruited.
2. Three employees have recently joined.
3. Two employees have attendance discrepancies.
4. One employee has submitted a grievance.
5. The hospital wants to introduce a training programme.
6. Management wants to reduce employee turnover.
What does HR do?
Situation	HR Responsibility
Five nurses required	Recruitment and selection
Three new employees joined	Onboarding
Attendance discrepancies	Attendance management
Employee grievance	Employee relations
Training programme	Learning and development
High employee turnover	Retention strategy


Practical Scenario
The hospital has experienced an increase in nurse resignations over the past three months.

Management asks HR to investigate.

An HR Executive should not immediately assume that employees are resigning because of salary dissatisfaction.

Instead, HR should:
1. Review resignation records.
2. Conduct or review exit interviews.
3. Examine attendance and overtime patterns.
4. Identify department-wise attrition.
5. Collect employee feedback.
6. Discuss findings with department heads.
7. Recommend appropriate corrective measures.
This demonstrates how HR uses employee information to support organisational decisions.
