TOPIC 1: INTRODUCTION TO THE EMPLOYEE LIFECYCLE
1.1 What is the Employee Lifecycle?
The employee lifecycle refers to the different stages an individual goes through during their relationship with an organisation.

It begins when an organisation identifies the need to hire someone and continues through recruitment, onboarding, development, retention and eventually separation.

HR manages and supports these stages to ensure that employees have a structured and positive experience throughout their employment.

1.2 Why is the Employee Lifecycle Important?
An organisation cannot focus only on hiring employees.

It must also ensure that employees:

Understand their responsibilities.
Receive proper training.
Get appropriate compensation.
Have opportunities for professional development.
Receive fair performance evaluations.
Work in a healthy environment.
Have a structured exit process when they leave.
If any stage is poorly managed, it can negatively affect employee satisfaction and organisational performance.

Example:

A hospital successfully recruits five nurses but fails to provide proper induction, shift information or workplace training.

Even though recruitment was successful, the employees may struggle to perform their responsibilities and could eventually resign.

This demonstrates why every stage of the employee lifecycle matters.

1.3 The Seven Stages of the Employee Lifecycle
For this curriculum, we will use the following seven-stage model:

Attraction.
Recruitment and Selection.
Onboarding.
Development.
Retention and Engagement.
Performance Management.
Separation.
Different organisations may combine or label these stages differently. The underlying activities remain broadly similar.

TOPIC 2: STAGE 1 – EMPLOYEE ATTRACTION
2.1 What is Employee Attraction?
Employee attraction refers to the activities an organisation undertakes to create awareness of employment opportunities and encourage suitable candidates to apply.

The objective is to make the organisation attractive to potential employees.

Before recruitment begins, candidates should have a reason to consider working for the organisation.

2.2 Methods of Employee Attraction
A. Employer Branding

Employer branding refers to how an organisation is perceived as a place to work.

It includes:

Company reputation.
Work environment.
Employee experiences.
Career opportunities.
Workplace culture.
Compensation and benefits.
Example:

A hospital known for providing professional training and a supportive working environment may attract more qualified nurses.

B. Job Advertisements

Organisations publish vacancies through different channels.

Common platforms include:

LinkedIn.
Naukri.
Indeed.
Foundit.
Internshala.
Company career websites.
C. Employee Referrals

Employee referrals involve existing employees recommending suitable candidates for vacancies.

Example:

An existing Biomedical Engineer recommends a qualified former colleague for an opening in the hospital's biomedical department.

D. Campus Recruitment

Organisations approach colleges and educational institutions to recruit students or recent graduates.

Example:

A hospital collaborates with a nursing college to recruit newly qualified nurses.

E. Social Media Recruitment

Organisations use professional and social media platforms to promote job opportunities.

2.3 Factors That Attract Candidates
Candidates may consider:

Salary and benefits.
Career growth.
Job security.
Work environment.
Organisation reputation.
Learning opportunities.
Work-life balance.
Location.
Leadership and management.
Practical Understanding
Suppose a hospital is struggling to attract qualified Biomedical Engineers.

HR should examine:

Whether the salary is competitive.
Whether the job description is clear.
Whether the organisation is reaching relevant candidates.
Whether the job location is suitable.
Whether the role offers professional development.
Key Point: Employee attraction is about making suitable candidates interested in working for the organisation. Recruitment is the process of converting that interest into actual applications and hiring.

TOPIC 3: STAGE 2 – RECRUITMENT AND SELECTION
3.1 What is Recruitment?
Recruitment is the process of identifying, attracting and encouraging suitable candidates to apply for job vacancies within an organisation.

It involves creating a candidate pool from which the organisation can select suitable employees.

3.2 What is Selection?
Selection is the process of evaluating candidates and choosing the most suitable person for a particular job.

Recruitment attracts candidates, while selection identifies the most appropriate candidate.

3.3 Difference Between Recruitment and Selection
Basis	Recruitment	Selection
Meaning	Attracting candidates	Choosing suitable candidates
Objective	Generate applications	Identify the best fit
Nature	Positive process of attracting applicants	Evaluative process of screening candidates
Activities	Job advertisements, sourcing, referrals	Interviews, assessments, verification
Outcome	Candidate pool	Selected candidate
3.4 Recruitment and Selection Process
Understand this sequence:

Step 1: Manpower Requirement

The organisation identifies the need for additional employees.

Example: A hospital needs three additional Lab Technicians.

Step 2: Job Analysis

HR gathers information about:

Job responsibilities.
Required qualifications.
Skills.
Experience.
Reporting structure.
Step 3: Job Description

HR prepares a document explaining the responsibilities and expectations of the position.

Step 4: Candidate Sourcing

HR identifies candidates through:

Job portals.
LinkedIn.
Referrals.
Campus recruitment.
Recruitment agencies.
Step 5: Resume Screening

Applications are reviewed against the job requirements.

Step 6: Interview Coordination

HR schedules interviews between shortlisted candidates and hiring managers.

Step 7: Candidate Evaluation

Candidates are assessed based on:

Qualifications.
Relevant experience.
Technical knowledge.
Communication.
Role suitability.
Step 8: Selection

The organisation selects the most suitable candidate.

Step 9: Background and Document Verification

Relevant educational, identity, employment and professional credentials are verified according to organisational policy and applicable requirements.

Step 10: Offer Management

The organisation communicates the employment offer, compensation and joining requirements.

Step 11: Joining Confirmation

The candidate accepts the offer and confirms the joining date.

3.5 Role of HR During Recruitment
An HR professional may be responsible for:

Understanding vacancy requirements.
Preparing job descriptions.
Publishing job advertisements.
Sourcing candidates.
Screening resumes.
Scheduling interviews.
Maintaining candidate trackers.
Coordinating feedback.
Communicating selection decisions.
Coordinating offer documentation.
Important: HR often coordinates the recruitment process, while technical selection decisions may involve the relevant department head.

TOPIC 4: STAGE 3 – EMPLOYEE ONBOARDING
4.1 What is Employee Onboarding?
Employee onboarding is the structured process of integrating a newly hired employee into an organisation.

It helps new employees understand their workplace, responsibilities, organisational policies and the resources required to perform their jobs.

Onboarding starts before or on the employee's joining date and continues through the initial period of employment.

4.2 Difference Between Joining and Onboarding
Joining refers to the employee officially beginning employment with the organisation.

Onboarding is the broader process of helping the employee settle into the organisation.

For example:

An employee may officially join on Monday, but their onboarding activities may continue over the following weeks.

4.3 Stages of Onboarding
A. Preboarding

Preboarding refers to activities completed after offer acceptance but before the employee's joining date.

Activities include:

Sending joining instructions.
Collecting required documents.
Confirming joining date.
Preparing workstation or equipment.
Informing the concerned department.
B. Joining Day

Activities include:

Document collection.
Identity verification.
Employment documentation.
Introduction to HR.
Introduction to the reporting manager.
Workplace orientation.
C. Induction

Induction introduces employees to the organisation.

It may include:

Organisation history.
Mission and vision.
HR policies.
Workplace rules.
Department structure.
Reporting procedures.
D. Departmental Orientation

Employees learn about their specific department and responsibilities.

For example, a newly hired Biomedical Engineer may receive an introduction to:

Biomedical equipment.
Maintenance procedures.
Equipment documentation.
Safety requirements.
Reporting hierarchy.
E. Initial Employee Support

HR and the reporting manager monitor whether the new employee is settling into the role.

This may involve:

Feedback sessions.
Training.
Clarification of responsibilities.
Performance discussions.
4.4 Important Onboarding Documents
Document	Purpose
Joining Form	Collects employee joining information
Identity Proof	Verifies identity
Address Proof	Records residential information
Educational Certificates	Supports qualification verification
Appointment Letter	Documents employment terms
Bank Details Form	Supports salary processing
Emergency Contact Form	Records emergency contact information
Employee Information Form	Maintains employee master data
Policy Acknowledgement	Confirms receipt or acceptance of relevant policies
The exact documents depend on the organisation and the role.

4.5 Why is Onboarding Important?
Effective onboarding helps:

Reduce confusion among new employees.
Improve employee confidence.
Explain organisational expectations.
Support faster adjustment to the role.
Reduce early employee turnover.
Improve employee experience.
Practical Example:

A new nurse joins a hospital.

If the hospital provides a structured induction explaining shift procedures, reporting hierarchy, safety protocols and department responsibilities, the nurse can understand the workplace more effectively.

TOPIC 5: STAGE 4 – EMPLOYEE DEVELOPMENT
5.1 What is Employee Development?
Employee development refers to activities that improve employees' knowledge, skills, abilities and professional capabilities over time.

It focuses on preparing employees to perform their current responsibilities effectively and potentially take on greater responsibilities in the future.

5.2 Training vs Development
These terms are related but different.

Basis	Training	Development
Focus	Specific job-related skills	Broader professional growth
Duration	Often short-term	Usually long-term
Objective	Improve current job performance	Prepare for future responsibilities
Approach	Specific and task-oriented	Broader and continuous
Example	Training an employee on HRMS	Preparing an employee for an HR Manager role
5.3 Types of Employee Training
A. Technical Training

Improves job-specific technical skills.

Example: Training a Biomedical Engineer to operate or maintain specific medical equipment.

B. Soft Skills Training

Improves interpersonal and communication abilities.

Examples:

Communication.
Teamwork.
Leadership.
Conflict management.
Time management.
C. Compliance Training

Helps employees understand relevant organisational rules and legal requirements.

Examples:

POSH awareness.
Workplace safety.
Data confidentiality.
Information security.
D. Induction Training

Introduces employees to the organisation, its culture and policies.

E. Leadership Training

Develops employees' ability to supervise teams, make decisions and manage responsibilities.

5.4 Training Needs Analysis (TNA)
Training Needs Analysis is the process of identifying the gap between the skills employees currently possess and the skills required to perform their jobs effectively.

Example:

A hospital introduces a new HRMS.

The HR department identifies that some employees do not understand how to submit leave requests through the system.

HR conducts a training session to address this skill gap.

5.5 Employee Career Development
Career development focuses on helping employees progress professionally.

It may include:

Career planning.
Mentorship.
Leadership programmes.
Skill development.
Job rotation.
Internal promotions.
Succession planning.
Key Point: Employee development benefits both the individual and the organisation by improving capabilities and supporting future workforce requirements.

TOPIC 6: STAGE 5 – EMPLOYEE ENGAGEMENT AND RETENTION
6.1 What is Employee Engagement?
Employee engagement refers to the level of commitment, involvement and connection employees have with their organisation and work.

Engaged employees generally show greater interest in contributing towards organisational objectives.

Engagement is not simply about whether an employee is happy. It also concerns their motivation, commitment and willingness to contribute.

6.2 Methods of Improving Employee Engagement
A. Recognition Programmes

Recognising employees for good performance and contributions.

Example: Employee of the Month.

B. Employee Feedback

Collecting employee opinions through surveys, meetings and feedback sessions.

C. Career Growth Opportunities

Providing employees with opportunities to learn and advance.

D. Workplace Communication

Maintaining transparent communication between management and employees.

E. Employee Welfare Initiatives

Supporting employee well-being through suitable workplace facilities and initiatives.

F. Team-Building Activities

Organising activities that encourage collaboration and stronger workplace relationships.

6.3 What is Employee Retention?
Employee retention refers to an organisation's ability to keep employees working within the organisation over a particular period.

A high retention rate generally indicates that a large proportion of employees continue working with the organisation.

6.4 Why is Employee Retention Important?
High employee turnover can create several problems:

Increased recruitment costs.
Additional training expenses.
Loss of organisational knowledge.
Reduced team stability.
Increased workload for existing employees.
Operational disruptions.
6.5 Factors Affecting Employee Retention
Employees may leave because of:

Low or uncompetitive compensation.
Limited career growth.
Poor management.
Excessive workload.
Unhealthy workplace culture.
Lack of recognition.
Poor work-life balance.
Better external opportunities.
HR should identify the actual reasons rather than assuming all resignations have the same cause.

6.6 Understanding Attrition
Employee attrition refers to the reduction in an organisation's workforce when employees leave and their positions are not immediately or permanently replaced.

A commonly used HR metric is the employee attrition rate.

Formula:

Attrition Rate = (Number of Employees Who Left During the Period ÷ Average Number of Employees During the Period) × 100

Where:

Average Headcount = (Opening Headcount + Closing Headcount) ÷ 2

Example:

A company has:

Opening employees: 100.
Closing employees: 90.
Employees who left during the period: 15.
Average Headcount:

(100 + 90) ÷ 2 = 95

Attrition Rate:

(15 ÷ 95) × 100 = 15.79%

Therefore, the attrition rate is approximately 15.79%.

Note that organisations may use different reporting conventions. The calculation should always state the measurement period and denominator used.

TOPIC 7: STAGE 6 – PERFORMANCE MANAGEMENT
7.1 What is Performance Management?
Performance management is a continuous process through which an organisation establishes expectations, monitors employee performance, provides feedback and supports improvement.

Its objective is to ensure that employees contribute effectively towards organisational goals.

7.2 Major Activities in Performance Management
A. Setting Performance Expectations

Employees should understand what they are expected to achieve.

Example:

An HR Executive is expected to maintain accurate employee records and complete onboarding documentation within specified timelines.

B. Establishing KRAs and KPIs

KRA stands for Key Result Area.

It defines the major responsibility or outcome expected from an employee.

KPI stands for Key Performance Indicator.

It is a measurable value used to assess performance.

Example:

KRA	KPI
Employee Onboarding	Percentage of onboarding tasks completed on time
Recruitment	Average time to fill vacancies
Employee Records	Employee record accuracy
Attendance	Attendance report accuracy
C. Performance Monitoring

Managers review employee performance through reports, discussions and observations.

D. Performance Appraisal

A formal evaluation of employee performance over a defined period.

E. Performance Improvement

Employees who are not meeting expectations may receive additional guidance, training or a Performance Improvement Plan, depending on organisational policy.

7.3 Why is Performance Management Important?
It helps:

Improve employee productivity.
Identify skill gaps.
Recognise good performance.
Support employee development.
Align individual work with organisational goals.
Improve accountability.
Practical Example:

An HR Executive repeatedly submits inaccurate employee attendance reports.

The manager reviews the errors, identifies the cause and provides additional training.

If performance continues to remain below expectations, a structured improvement process may be initiated.

TOPIC 8: STAGE 7 – EMPLOYEE SEPARATION
8.1 What is Employee Separation?
Employee separation refers to the end of the employment relationship between an employee and an organisation.

It can occur for several reasons, including resignation, retirement, termination or other employment-related circumstances.

8.2 Types of Employee Separation
A. Resignation

An employee voluntarily decides to leave the organisation.

Example: An employee resigns after receiving another job offer.

B. Retirement

An employee leaves employment after reaching the applicable retirement age or under an approved retirement arrangement.

C. Termination

The employer ends the employment relationship in accordance with applicable law, employment terms and organisational procedures.

D. Layoff or Retrenchment

These are distinct employment-related situations involving workforce reduction or termination under applicable legal conditions.

E. End of Contract

An employee's employment ends when a fixed-term or contractual arrangement expires, subject to the relevant terms and law.

8.3 Employee Exit Process
When an employee leaves, HR coordinates the exit process.

Typical steps include:

Step 1: Resignation Submission

The employee submits a resignation through the prescribed process.

Step 2: Resignation Acknowledgement

HR acknowledges receipt and coordinates the required formalities.

Step 3: Notice Period

The employee serves the applicable notice period or follows an approved alternative arrangement.

Step 4: Knowledge Transfer

The employee transfers relevant work information and responsibilities.

Step 5: Asset Clearance

The employee returns company-owned items such as:

Laptop.
ID card.
Access card.
Equipment.
Documents.
Step 6: Exit Interview

HR may conduct an exit interview to understand the employee's experience and reasons for leaving.

Step 7: Full and Final Settlement

The organisation calculates and processes the employee's final dues and applicable deductions.

Step 8: Exit Documentation

HR coordinates relevant employment documents according to organisational policy and applicable requirements.

8.4 What is an Exit Interview?
An exit interview is a discussion between an organisation and an employee who is leaving.

Its purpose is to understand the employee's experience and identify possible organisational improvements.

Common questions include:

What is the primary reason for leaving?
How would you describe your experience?
Were your responsibilities clearly defined?
Did you receive adequate support from your manager?
Were there sufficient career development opportunities?
What improvements would you suggest?
8.5 Importance of Proper Separation Management
A structured exit process helps:

Ensure proper documentation.
Protect organisational assets.
Support knowledge transfer.
Identify employee concerns.
Coordinate final settlement.
Maintain professional relationships.
TOPIC 9: COMPLETE EMPLOYEE LIFECYCLE – PRACTICAL CASE STUDY
Now let's connect all seven stages.

Case Study: A Hospital Hiring a Biomedical Engineer
A hospital identifies a requirement for one Biomedical Engineer to manage its medical equipment.

Follow the employee through the complete lifecycle.

Stage	Activity	HR Responsibility
1. Attraction	Hospital advertises the vacancy	Promote the opportunity
2. Recruitment & Selection	Candidates apply and interviews take place	Source, screen and coordinate selection
3. Onboarding	Selected candidate joins	Collect documents and coordinate induction
4. Development	Employee receives technical training	Coordinate development activities
5. Engagement & Retention	Employee receives feedback and career support	Support employee experience
6. Performance Management	Manager evaluates employee performance	Coordinate appraisal process
7. Separation	Employee resigns after three years	Coordinate exit formalities
Scenario Analysis
Suppose the Biomedical Engineer resigns after three years.

HR reviews the employee's records and discovers:

The employee received no formal career development discussion.
The employee had requested additional training twice.
The employee's workload had increased.
The employee mentioned limited career progression during the exit interview.
What can HR learn?

The organisation may need to improve:

Career development planning.
Training opportunities.
Workload management.
Employee feedback mechanisms.
This demonstrates how information from the separation stage can help improve earlier stages of the employee lifecycle.

Important Concept: The employee lifecycle is not just a sequence of administrative activities. Information collected at one stage can help an organisation improve its decisions at other stages.
