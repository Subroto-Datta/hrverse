TOPIC 1: INTRODUCTION TO HR POLICIES
1.1 What is an HR Policy?
An HR policy is a formal guideline established by an organisation to regulate employee-related matters and ensure consistency in workplace decisions and practices.

It explains what employees and management are expected to do in specific situations.

In simple terms, HR policies establish the rules and expectations employees must follow during their employment.

Practical Example
Consider a company with 200 employees.

Without an attendance policy:

Employees may follow different reporting times.
Managers may approve leave inconsistently.
Late arrivals may not be recorded properly.
Attendance disputes may arise.
Employees may not understand the consequences of repeated absence.
An attendance policy establishes a common framework that employees and managers must follow.

1.2 Objectives of HR Policies
HR policies are created to achieve the following objectives:

A. Consistency

Ensures that similar employee situations are handled using consistent organisational standards.

Example: Leave requests are evaluated according to a common approval process.

B. Transparency

Employees understand workplace expectations, entitlements and responsibilities.

Example: Employees know how to apply for annual leave and whom to approach for approval.

C. Legal Compliance

Policies help organisations implement applicable employment laws and regulations.

Example: An organisation establishes procedures for addressing workplace sexual harassment complaints in accordance with the POSH Act.

D. Employee Protection

Policies establish safeguards for employees.

Example: A workplace anti-harassment policy explains reporting channels and expected organisational responses.

E. Organisational Discipline

Policies establish expected standards of workplace behaviour.

Example: Employees are required to follow attendance procedures and maintain professional conduct.

F. Efficient Decision-Making

Managers and HR teams can refer to established policies instead of making inconsistent decisions.

TOPIC 2: DIFFERENCE BETWEEN POLICIES, PROCEDURES, RULES AND GUIDELINES
These four terms are related but have different meanings.

2.1 Policy
A policy explains an organisation's overall position, principles or approach towards a particular matter.

Example:

Leave Policy: Employees may request leave according to their eligibility and the organisation's prescribed approval process.

2.2 Procedure
A procedure explains the steps that must be followed to implement a policy.

Example:

An employee must:

Log in to the HRMS.
Select the leave category.
Enter the requested dates.
Submit the application.
Wait for the reporting manager's decision.
2.3 Rule
A rule specifies a requirement or restriction that employees are expected to follow.

Example:

Employees must wear their official identity cards while inside the organisation's premises.

2.4 Guideline
A guideline provides recommendations or instructions to help employees make appropriate decisions.

Example:

Employees are encouraged to communicate professionally and respond to work-related emails within a reasonable period.

Comparison
Basis	Policy	Procedure	Rule	Guideline
Meaning	Establishes organisational principles	Explains steps	Specifies requirements	Provides recommendations
Purpose	Consistency	Implementation	Discipline	Guidance
Nature	Broad	Operational	Specific	Advisory
Example	Leave Policy	Leave Application Process	ID Card Requirement	Email Etiquette
Remember: A policy explains what the organisation expects. A procedure explains how to implement it.

TOPIC 3: MAJOR TYPES OF HR POLICIES
Organisations may have several HR policies depending on their size, industry and legal requirements.

Let's understand the most important ones.

3.1 Attendance Policy
An attendance policy establishes expectations regarding employee attendance and reporting time.

It generally covers:

Working hours.
Reporting time.
Late arrivals.
Early departures.
Absence reporting.
Attendance recording.
Shift attendance.
Regularisation of attendance.
Consequences of unauthorised absence.
Example:

An organisation has working hours from 9:30 AM to 6:30 PM.

Its attendance policy explains:

How employees record attendance.
How late arrivals are recorded.
Whom employees should inform if they cannot attend work.
How attendance corrections are requested.
HR Responsibility:

Monitor attendance records.
Identify discrepancies.
Coordinate corrections.
Prepare attendance reports.
Escalate repeated attendance issues according to policy.
3.2 Leave Policy
A leave policy explains the types of leave available to employees and the process for requesting and approving leave.

Common categories include:

A. Casual Leave (CL)

Generally provided for short-term personal requirements.

Example: An employee needs a day off for personal work.

B. Sick Leave (SL)

Used when an employee cannot work due to illness or health-related reasons.

Example: An employee requires leave due to fever.

C. Earned Leave / Privilege Leave (EL/PL)

Leave accumulated according to the organisation's policy and applicable rules.

Example: An employee plans a holiday and applies for previously accrued leave.

D. Maternity Leave

Leave provided to eligible employees in accordance with applicable maternity benefit provisions.

E. Paternity Leave

Leave provided to eligible employees according to organisational policy or applicable service rules.

F. Leave Without Pay (LWP)

Leave taken when paid leave is unavailable, exhausted or otherwise not applicable, subject to approval and policy.

G. Compensatory Off (Comp Off)

Time off granted under applicable organisational rules for eligible additional work performed on holidays or weekly offs.

Important HR Responsibility
HR must maintain accurate leave records and ensure that leave administration follows applicable laws, employment terms and organisational policies.

Important: Leave entitlements are not identical across all organisations. Statutory requirements, state-specific rules, establishment type and employment terms must be considered.

3.3 Work From Home Policy
A Work From Home (WFH) policy explains the conditions under which employees may work remotely.

It may cover:

Eligibility.
Approval requirements.
Working hours.
Attendance recording.
Productivity expectations.
Data security.
Communication.
Equipment requirements.
Example:

A company permits eligible employees to work remotely two days per week with prior managerial approval.

3.4 Code of Conduct Policy
A Code of Conduct establishes the professional and ethical behaviour expected from employees.

It may cover:

Respectful workplace behaviour.
Professional communication.
Workplace discipline.
Conflict of interest.
Confidentiality.
Anti-harassment expectations.
Use of company resources.
Ethical decision-making.
Example:

Employees must not disclose confidential company information to unauthorised individuals.

3.5 Anti-Harassment and POSH Policy
Organisations must establish appropriate mechanisms to prevent and address workplace sexual harassment.

In India, the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013, commonly called the POSH Act, establishes important obligations for employers.

A POSH policy should explain:

Prohibited conduct.
Complaint channels.
Internal Committee details, where applicable.
Complaint-handling procedures.
Confidentiality.
Awareness and training.
Protection against retaliation.
Appropriate action under applicable law.
Important: HR must not independently conduct a POSH inquiry outside the legally prescribed process. The Internal Committee or Local Committee handles complaints within its jurisdiction under the applicable statutory framework.

3.6 Disciplinary Policy
A disciplinary policy explains how an organisation addresses employee misconduct or violations of workplace rules.

It may cover:

Types of misconduct.
Reporting procedures.
Preliminary assessment.
Investigation requirements.
Employee opportunity to respond.
Disciplinary action.
Appeal or review mechanisms, where applicable.
Example:

An employee repeatedly violates workplace safety procedures.

The organisation follows its established disciplinary process and applicable legal requirements.

3.7 Performance Management Policy
This policy establishes how employee performance will be evaluated.

It may include:

Performance review frequency.
KRA and KPI setting.
Evaluation criteria.
Feedback mechanisms.
Appraisal procedures.
Performance improvement processes.
Documentation requirements.
3.8 Recruitment Policy
A recruitment policy establishes how the organisation hires employees.

It may cover:

Vacancy approval.
Job descriptions.
Candidate sourcing.
Resume screening.
Interview procedures.
Candidate evaluation.
Selection approvals.
Offer management.
Background verification.
3.9 Confidentiality and Data Protection Policy
This policy establishes how employees must handle confidential and personal information.

It may cover:

Employee personal information.
Salary information.
Customer or patient information.
Company documents.
Passwords and system access.
Data sharing.
Document storage.
Information security incidents.
Healthcare Example:

A hospital employee must not share patient medical records with unauthorised individuals.

Similarly, HR employees must not casually disclose another employee's salary, personal details or grievance records.

TOPIC 4: UNDERSTANDING WORKPLACE DISCIPLINE
4.1 What is Workplace Discipline?
Workplace discipline refers to maintaining expected standards of employee behaviour, attendance, professionalism and compliance with organisational rules.

Its objective is to establish a structured environment where employees understand their responsibilities and conduct themselves appropriately.

4.2 Why is Workplace Discipline Important?
Workplace discipline helps:

Maintain productivity.
Reduce workplace conflicts.
Improve employee accountability.
Establish professional behaviour.
Protect organisational resources.
Maintain workplace safety.
Ensure consistent operations.
4.3 Examples of Workplace Misconduct
Examples may include:

Repeated unauthorised absence.
Workplace harassment.
Deliberate falsification of attendance records.
Misuse of organisational property.
Unauthorised disclosure of confidential information.
Serious violation of workplace safety procedures.
Insubordination or refusal to follow lawful and reasonable instructions.
The seriousness of an incident must be assessed based on facts, applicable rules and relevant legal requirements.

4.4 How Should HR Handle a Disciplinary Issue?
Consider this situation:

An employee repeatedly arrives late without providing an explanation.

HR should follow an appropriate process:

Step 1: Identify the Issue

Review attendance records and confirm the discrepancy.

Step 2: Verify Information

Check whether there are genuine attendance-recording errors or approved exceptions.

Step 3: Communicate with the Employee

Ask the employee to explain the situation.

Step 4: Review the Explanation

Understand the employee's circumstances and any supporting information.

Step 5: Follow Organisational Policy

Apply the relevant policy consistently and proportionately.

Step 6: Document the Matter

Maintain appropriate records of communication and action taken.

Step 7: Follow Up

Monitor whether the issue has been resolved.

Important Principle
HR should not immediately punish an employee based on an allegation.

It must establish the facts, provide an appropriate opportunity to respond and follow the organisation's disciplinary procedure and applicable law.

TOPIC 5: ORGANISATIONAL CULTURE
5.1 What is Organisational Culture?
Organisational culture refers to the shared values, beliefs, attitudes, behaviours and working practices that influence how people interact and perform within an organisation.

In simple terms, organisational culture describes how things are generally done within a workplace.

5.2 Elements of Organisational Culture
A. Values

The principles an organisation considers important.

Examples:

Integrity.
Respect.
Accountability.
Innovation.
Teamwork.
B. Beliefs

The shared assumptions that influence workplace decisions and behaviour.

Example:

An organisation believes that employee development improves long-term business performance.

C. Behaviour

The way employees and managers act in the workplace.

Example:

Employees communicate respectfully and support one another during challenging situations.

D. Leadership Style

The way managers guide, supervise and interact with employees.

Example:

A manager who encourages open communication may create a more participative working environment.

E. Communication

How information flows within the organisation.

Example:

An organisation regularly conducts employee meetings and shares important workplace updates.

F. Workplace Environment

The physical, social and psychological conditions in which employees work.

5.3 Types of Organisational Culture
A useful model developed by researchers Robert Quinn and Kim Cameron identifies four broad organisational culture types.

1. Clan Culture

Focuses on collaboration, teamwork and employee relationships.

Characteristics:

Supportive leadership.
Teamwork.
Employee participation.
Strong interpersonal relationships.
Example:

A hospital where departments work closely together and encourage staff collaboration.

2. Adhocracy Culture

Focuses on innovation, experimentation and adaptability.

Characteristics:

Creativity.
Innovation.
Risk-taking.
Flexible working practices.
Example:

A technology startup that encourages employees to experiment with new product ideas.

3. Market Culture

Focuses on performance, competition and achieving measurable results.

Characteristics:

Target-oriented work.
Performance measurement.
Competitive environment.
Business growth.
Example:

A sales organisation that evaluates employees against revenue targets.

4. Hierarchy Culture

Focuses on structure, formal processes, rules and organisational control.

Characteristics:

Defined reporting structures.
Formal procedures.
Standardisation.
Clear responsibilities.
Example:

A highly regulated organisation where employees must follow documented procedures.

Comparison
Culture Type	Main Focus	Key Characteristics
Clan	Collaboration	Teamwork and relationships
Adhocracy	Innovation	Creativity and adaptability
Market	Results	Targets and competition
Hierarchy	Structure	Rules and procedures
Organisations may combine elements of multiple culture types.

5.4 Role of HR in Developing Organisational Culture
HR contributes to organisational culture through:

Recruitment practices.
Employee induction.
Workplace policies.
Leadership development.
Employee engagement.
Recognition programmes.
Performance management.
Grievance handling.
Internal communication.
Practical Example:

An organisation wants to establish a culture of accountability.

HR can support this by helping management define responsibilities, introduce measurable performance expectations and establish transparent feedback mechanisms.

TOPIC 6: PROFESSIONAL WORKPLACE BEHAVIOUR AND ETHICS
6.1 What is Professional Behaviour?
Professional behaviour refers to the conduct expected from individuals while performing their workplace responsibilities.

It involves acting responsibly, respectfully and ethically.

6.2 Important Elements
A. Punctuality

Arriving on time and meeting work-related deadlines.

B. Respect

Treating colleagues, managers and employees respectfully.

C. Accountability

Accepting responsibility for assigned tasks and mistakes.

D. Integrity

Being honest and avoiding unethical practices.

E. Confidentiality

Protecting sensitive organisational and employee information.

F. Communication

Using clear, respectful and appropriate language.

G. Teamwork

Cooperating with colleagues to achieve common objectives.

6.3 Workplace Ethics
Workplace ethics refers to the moral principles and standards that guide behaviour and decision-making within an organisation.

Examples include:

Honesty.
Fairness.
Respect.
Transparency.
Responsibility.
Confidentiality.
Non-discrimination.
6.4 Ethical Challenges in HR
HR professionals may face situations involving:

Situation 1: Salary Confidentiality

An employee asks an HR Executive to disclose another employee's salary.

The HR Executive should not disclose confidential salary information without proper authorisation.

Situation 2: Unfair Recruitment

A manager requests that an unqualified personal acquaintance be selected over more suitable candidates.

HR should follow the established selection criteria and approval procedures.

Situation 3: Employee Grievance

An employee reports inappropriate workplace behaviour by a senior manager.

HR should take the complaint seriously and follow the appropriate reporting and investigation procedures.

Situation 4: Personal Conflict of Interest

An HR professional participates in the selection process for a close relative.

The relationship should be disclosed, and the professional should follow the organisation's conflict-of-interest procedure.

TOPIC 7: CONFIDENTIALITY IN HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT
This deserves special attention because HR professionals have access to highly sensitive employee information.

7.1 What is Confidentiality?
Confidentiality refers to protecting information from unauthorised access, disclosure or misuse.

HR professionals routinely handle:

Employee addresses.
Identity information.
Bank details.
Salary information.
Medical or leave-related records.
Performance evaluations.
Disciplinary records.
Grievances.
Recruitment information.
7.2 Why is Confidentiality Important?
It helps:

Protect employee privacy.
Maintain organisational trust.
Prevent misuse of personal information.
Reduce data security risks.
Support compliance with applicable data protection requirements.
7.3 Practical Rules for HR Professionals
An HR professional should:

Share employee records only with authorised individuals.
Avoid discussing confidential matters in public.
Use approved organisational systems.
Maintain secure document storage.
Follow access-control requirements.
Avoid sending sensitive records to unauthorised email addresses.
Report suspected data breaches through appropriate channels.
Healthcare Example:

A hospital HR Executive should not disclose an employee's medical information to unrelated colleagues simply because they are curious.

Similarly, access to patient information should remain restricted to authorised individuals who need it for legitimate purposes.

TOPIC 8: PRACTICAL CASE STUDY – DESIGNING HR POLICIES FOR A HOSPITAL
Let's apply everything.

Situation
A hospital has 100 employees.

Management has identified the following problems:

Employees frequently arrive late.
Leave applications are submitted without a consistent process.
Employees are unaware of workplace behaviour expectations.
Some employees share confidential information.
Workplace conflicts are increasing.
Employees are unsure how to raise complaints.
Management asks the HR department to establish a basic policy framework.

Required HR Policies
Problem	Required Policy
Late arrivals	Attendance Policy
Unstructured leave applications	Leave Policy
Unprofessional conduct	Code of Conduct
Information disclosure	Confidentiality Policy
Workplace conflicts	Grievance and Employee Relations Procedure
Harassment complaints	POSH Policy and Complaint Procedure
How Should HR Approach This?
Step 1: Identify the problems and collect relevant information.

Step 2: Review applicable laws and existing organisational requirements.

Step 3: Draft suitable policies.

Step 4: Obtain management and relevant legal approval.

Step 5: Communicate policies to employees.

Step 6: Conduct employee awareness sessions.

Step 7: Monitor implementation.

Step 8: Review policies periodically and update them when necessary.

Key Understanding: Creating a policy is not enough. Employees must understand it, management must implement it consistently and HR must monitor whether it is effective.
