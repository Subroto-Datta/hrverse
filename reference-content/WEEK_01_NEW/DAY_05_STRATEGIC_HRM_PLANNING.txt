TOPIC 1: INTRODUCTION TO STRATEGIC HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT
1.1 What is Strategic Human Resource Management?
Strategic Human Resource Management (SHRM) refers to the process of aligning an organisation's HR policies, practices and workforce capabilities with its long-term business objectives.

In simple terms, strategic HRM ensures that an organisation has the right people, skills and workforce structure to achieve its future goals.

Traditional HR activities often focus on immediate employee-related requirements.

Strategic HRM looks beyond immediate requirements and considers how people management contributes to long-term organisational success.

Example
Consider a hospital that currently has 100 employees.

Management plans to expand its operations and open two additional branches within three years.

This expansion will require:

Additional doctors.
More nurses.
Biomedical Engineers.
Lab Technicians.
Hospital Administrators.
HR professionals.
Finance and administrative employees.
If HR starts recruiting only after the new branches open, the hospital may experience staffing shortages.

Instead, strategic HR planning anticipates these requirements and prepares a workforce strategy in advance.

This is an example of Strategic Human Resource Management.

1.2 Objectives of Strategic HRM
A. Aligning HR with Business Goals

HR activities should support the organisation's business objectives.

Example:

If a company wants to expand its sales operations, HR must plan recruitment and training for additional sales employees.

B. Improving Workforce Productivity

HR works towards ensuring that employees have the necessary skills, resources and support to perform effectively.

C. Developing Talent

Organisations need employees with suitable capabilities to meet future requirements.

HR supports this through training, career development and succession planning.

D. Improving Employee Retention

Strategic HRM considers employee satisfaction, engagement and retention as important factors in organisational stability.

E. Managing Workforce Costs

HR helps management plan recruitment budgets, compensation structures and workforce expenditure.

F. Preparing for Organisational Changes

Organisations may experience expansion, restructuring, technological changes or new business requirements.

HR helps prepare employees and workforce structures for these changes.

TOPIC 2: DIFFERENCE BETWEEN TRADITIONAL HRM AND STRATEGIC HRM
Understanding this difference is important.

Basis	Traditional HRM	Strategic HRM
Focus	Routine employee administration	Long-term workforce strategy
Approach	Primarily operational	Strategic and future-oriented
Planning	Immediate requirements	Future workforce requirements
Employee Development	Job-related training	Long-term capability development
Decision-Making	Mainly HR operational decisions	Integrated with business decisions
Recruitment	Filling current vacancies	Building future talent pipelines
Performance	Monitoring individual performance	Aligning performance with business goals
Example	Hiring five nurses for current vacancies	Planning workforce requirements for future hospital expansion
Important: Traditional HR activities are not unnecessary. Strategic HRM builds on operational HR processes and connects them with organisational objectives.

TOPIC 3: UNDERSTANDING HUMAN RESOURCE PLANNING (HRP)
3.1 What is Human Resource Planning?
Human Resource Planning (HRP), also called workforce planning, is the process of identifying an organisation's current and future employee requirements and ensuring that suitable employees are available when needed.

It helps answer four important questions:

How many employees does the organisation currently have?
How many employees will it need in the future?
What skills and qualifications will be required?
How will the organisation fulfil the workforce gap?
3.2 Objectives of HR Planning
A. Ensuring Adequate Staffing

HR planning ensures that the organisation has sufficient employees to perform its operations.

B. Avoiding Workforce Shortages

It helps organisations anticipate situations where employee availability may be insufficient.

C. Avoiding Workforce Surplus

An organisation may also have more employees than required.

HR planning helps identify and manage such situations.

D. Identifying Skill Gaps

It identifies differences between existing employee capabilities and future job requirements.

E. Supporting Organisational Expansion

HR planning prepares the workforce needed for new branches, departments or business operations.

F. Controlling Recruitment Costs

Advance planning can reduce urgent recruitment expenses and inefficient hiring.

TOPIC 4: THE HUMAN RESOURCE PLANNING PROCESS
Human Resource Planning generally involves the following stages.

Step 1: Analyse Organisational Objectives
HR first understands the organisation's business plans.

Example:

A hospital plans to expand its ICU capacity from 10 beds to 25 beds.

This expansion will require additional medical and support staff.

Step 2: Analyse Existing Workforce
HR studies the current workforce.

Important information includes:

Total employee headcount.
Department-wise employee count.
Employee designations.
Qualifications.
Skills.
Experience.
Retirement or resignation projections.
Employee availability.
This is called workforce analysis.

Step 3: Forecast Future Workforce Demand
HR estimates the number and types of employees required in the future.

For example:

Department	Current Employees	Future Requirement
Nursing	30	45
Laboratory	10	14
Biomedical	5	8
Administration	15	18
Finance	5	6
These numbers are illustrative assumptions.

Step 4: Forecast Workforce Supply
HR estimates how many employees will be available to fulfil future requirements.

Supply can come from:

Internal Sources

Existing employees.
Promotions.
Transfers.
Internal role changes.
Employees returning from approved leave.
External Sources

New recruitment.
Campus placements.
Recruitment agencies.
Employee referrals.
Job portals.
Professional networks.
Step 5: Identify Workforce Gaps
HR compares future workforce demand with expected workforce supply.

Formula:

Workforce Gap = Forecasted Workforce Demand − Forecasted Workforce Supply

Example:

A hospital requires 15 nurses but expects only 11 to be available.

Workforce Gap:

15 − 11 = 4 nurses.

Therefore, the hospital has a shortage of four nurses.

Step 6: Develop an Action Plan
HR decides how to address the workforce gap.

Possible actions:

Recruitment.
Internal transfers.
Promotions.
Training.
Reskilling.
Temporary staffing.
Workforce restructuring.
Step 7: Implement and Monitor
HR implements the plan and regularly reviews whether staffing requirements are being fulfilled.

TOPIC 5: WORKFORCE DEMAND AND SUPPLY FORECASTING
5.1 What is Workforce Demand Forecasting?
Workforce demand forecasting is the process of estimating how many employees and what types of skills an organisation will require in the future.

It considers factors such as:

Business expansion.
Increased workload.
New projects.
Employee productivity.
Technology changes.
Departmental requirements.
Expected employee turnover.
Example
A hospital currently has five Biomedical Engineers.

Management plans to install additional medical equipment and expand its ICU.

The department head estimates that eight Biomedical Engineers will be required next year.

Therefore:

Current Workforce = 5

Future Demand = 8

Additional Requirement = 3

HR must plan how to fulfil this requirement.

5.2 What is Workforce Supply Forecasting?
Workforce supply forecasting estimates the number of employees who will be available to fulfil future workforce requirements.

It considers:

Existing employees.
Expected resignations.
Retirements.
Promotions.
Transfers.
Internal training.
External recruitment availability.
Example
A hospital currently has 20 nurses.

During the next year:

Two nurses are expected to retire.
One nurse is expected to transfer to another branch.
Three new nurses are expected to join.
Expected workforce supply:

20 − 2 − 1 + 3 = 20 nurses.

If future demand is 25 nurses:

Workforce Gap = 25 − 20 = 5 nurses.

The hospital must arrange additional staffing.

5.3 Difference Between Demand and Supply Forecasting
Basis	Demand Forecasting	Supply Forecasting
Meaning	Estimates employees required	Estimates employees available
Focus	Future workforce needs	Future workforce availability
Sources	Business plans and workload	Existing employees and labour market
Objective	Determine staffing requirements	Determine staffing availability
Outcome	Required headcount	Expected available headcount
TOPIC 6: METHODS OF HUMAN RESOURCE PLANNING
There are different methods organisations use to estimate workforce requirements.

6.1 Managerial Judgement
Department heads and managers estimate future staffing requirements based on their experience and operational needs.

Example:

A Nursing Supervisor estimates that the department requires five additional nurses to manage increased patient workload.

6.2 Trend Analysis
Trend analysis uses historical workforce data to estimate future requirements.

Example:

A hospital's nursing department has increased its workforce by approximately 10% annually over the last three years.

HR may use this trend as one input when estimating future staffing requirements.

However, historical trends should not be applied blindly if business conditions change.

6.3 Ratio Analysis
Ratio analysis estimates staffing requirements using a relationship between two variables.

Example:

A hospital requires one administrative employee for every 20 employees.

If the hospital has 200 employees:

Required Administrative Employees:

200 ÷ 20 = 10.

6.4 Workload Analysis
Workload analysis estimates staffing requirements based on the amount of work that must be completed.

Example:

A recruitment team must process 300 applications every month.

If one recruiter can effectively process 100 applications per month:

Required Recruiters:

300 ÷ 100 = 3 recruiters.

6.5 Skills Inventory
A skills inventory is a record of employee qualifications, experience, knowledge and competencies.

It helps HR understand what capabilities already exist within the organisation.

Example:

A hospital has five Biomedical Engineers.

Two specialise in imaging equipment, while three have experience with ICU and monitoring equipment.

The hospital can use this information when planning future technical requirements.

TOPIC 7: UNDERSTANDING HR METRICS
Now we move to the analytical part of today's lesson.

HR metrics are measurable values used to evaluate workforce-related activities and HR performance.

They help organisations understand whether HR processes are working effectively.

For example:

Are employees leaving frequently?
How long does recruitment take?
Is absenteeism increasing?
How much does hiring cost?
Are employees staying with the organisation?
These questions can be answered using HR metrics.

7.1 Employee Headcount
Headcount refers to the total number of employees working in an organisation at a particular point in time.

Formula:

Employee Headcount = Total Number of Active Employees

Example:

A company has:

25 employees in HR.
50 employees in Sales.
30 employees in Finance.
45 employees in Operations.
Total Headcount:

25 + 50 + 30 + 45 = 150 employees.

HR Application: Helps management understand the size and distribution of the workforce.

7.2 Employee Attrition Rate
Attrition rate measures the percentage of the average workforce that leaves an organisation during a specified period.

Formula:

Attrition Rate = (Number of Employees Who Left ÷ Average Headcount) × 100

Average Headcount:

(Opening Headcount + Closing Headcount) ÷ 2

Example:

Opening Headcount = 100

Closing Headcount = 90

Employees Who Left = 15

Average Headcount:

(100 + 90) ÷ 2 = 95

Attrition Rate:

(15 ÷ 95) × 100 = 15.79%

Interpretation: Approximately 15.79% of the average workforce left during the specified period.

7.3 Employee Retention Rate
Retention rate measures the percentage of employees from the opening workforce who remain employed at the end of the period.

Formula:

Retention Rate = [(Employees at the End − New Employees Joined During the Period) ÷ Employees at the Beginning] × 100

Example:

Opening Headcount = 100

Closing Headcount = 90

New Employees Joined = 10

Retention Rate:

[(90 − 10) ÷ 100] × 100

= 80%

Therefore, the retention rate is 80%.

Important: Retention and attrition measure different aspects of workforce movement. They are not necessarily exact complements because they use different calculation conventions.

7.4 Absenteeism Rate
Absenteeism rate measures the proportion of scheduled working time lost due to employee absence.

Formula:

Absenteeism Rate = (Total Absent Workdays ÷ Total Scheduled Workdays) × 100

Example:

A department has 20 employees.

Each employee is scheduled to work 22 days in a month.

Total Scheduled Workdays:

20 × 22 = 440 days.

Total Absent Workdays = 22 days.

Absenteeism Rate:

(22 ÷ 440) × 100 = 5%

Interpretation: Employees were absent for 5% of the total scheduled working days.

The organisation should clearly define which absence categories are included in this metric.

7.5 Time to Hire
Time to Hire measures the time taken to hire a candidate, generally from the date the candidate enters the recruitment process to offer acceptance.

Organisations may use different starting and ending points, so the measurement definition should be consistent.

Formula:

Time to Hire = Offer Acceptance Date − Candidate Entry Date

Example:

Candidate enters the recruitment process on 1 November.

Candidate accepts the offer on 16 November.

Time to Hire = 15 days.

HR Application: Helps evaluate recruitment efficiency.

7.6 Time to Fill
Time to Fill measures the number of days taken to fill a vacancy, generally from the date the position is approved or opened until the selected candidate accepts the offer.

Formula:

Time to Fill = Offer Acceptance Date − Vacancy Approval Date

Example:

Vacancy approved on 1 November.

Candidate accepts the offer on 25 November.

Time to Fill = 24 days.

Time to Hire vs Time to Fill
Basis	Time to Hire	Time to Fill
Starting Point	Candidate enters recruitment process	Vacancy is approved or opened
Ending Point	Offer acceptance	Offer acceptance
Focus	Candidate recruitment experience	Overall vacancy-filling duration
7.7 Cost per Hire
Cost per Hire measures the average expenditure incurred to recruit one employee.

Formula:

Cost per Hire = Total Recruitment Costs ÷ Number of Employees Hired

Recruitment costs may include:

Job advertisements.
Recruitment agency fees.
Background verification.
Recruitment software.
Campus recruitment expenses.
Interview-related expenses.
Example:

Total recruitment expenditure = ₹60,000.

Employees hired = 5.

Cost per Hire:

₹60,000 ÷ 5 = ₹12,000.

Average Cost per Hire = ₹12,000.

7.8 Offer Acceptance Rate
Offer Acceptance Rate measures the percentage of job offers accepted by candidates.

Formula:

Offer Acceptance Rate = (Offers Accepted ÷ Total Offers Extended) × 100

Example:

Offers Extended = 20

Offers Accepted = 16

Offer Acceptance Rate:

(16 ÷ 20) × 100 = 80%.

Interpretation: 80% of the offers were accepted.

7.9 Employee Turnover Rate
Employee turnover measures the rate at which employees leave an organisation during a particular period.

In many HR reports, turnover and attrition are used interchangeably. However, some organisations distinguish them based on whether vacancies are replaced.

Always check the organisation's metric definition before interpreting the figure.

7.10 Training Effectiveness
Training effectiveness measures whether a training programme achieved its intended learning or performance outcomes.

Possible indicators include:

Training completion rate.
Assessment score improvement.
Skill improvement.
Employee feedback.
Reduction in work-related errors.
Improvement in productivity.
Example:

Before training, employees achieved an average assessment score of 60%.

After training, the average score increased to 85%.

Improvement:

85 − 60 = 25 percentage points.

This indicates an improvement in assessment performance, although additional evidence would be needed to establish whether the training caused improvements in actual workplace performance.

TOPIC 8: USING HR METRICS FOR MANAGEMENT DECISIONS
Calculating metrics is only the first step.

An HR professional must understand what the numbers indicate and what actions may be appropriate.

Example 1: High Attrition
A hospital's nursing department reports an attrition rate of 22%.

HR should investigate:

Salary competitiveness.
Shift allocation.
Workload.
Employee feedback.
Managerial practices.
Career development opportunities.
Possible actions:

Review shift scheduling.
Conduct employee feedback sessions.
Improve training and development.
Review compensation structures.
Strengthen employee support systems.
Example 2: High Absenteeism
A department reports an absenteeism rate of 12%.

HR should examine:

Absence patterns.
Department-wise absenteeism.
Shift-related issues.
Workload.
Employee welfare.
Attendance-record accuracy.
Possible actions should be based on the actual reasons identified.

Example 3: High Recruitment Time
A company takes an average of 45 days to fill vacancies.

HR should investigate:

Delayed interview scheduling.
Slow managerial feedback.
Limited candidate sourcing.
Unrealistic job requirements.
Offer approval delays.
Example 4: Low Offer Acceptance Rate
A company extends 30 offers but only 15 candidates accept.

Offer Acceptance Rate:

(15 ÷ 30) × 100 = 50%.

HR should investigate:

Compensation expectations.
Candidate experience.
Offer delays.
Job role clarity.
Competing offers.
Location and work arrangements.
Key Concept: HR metrics help identify problems. HR professionals must investigate the reasons before recommending solutions.

TOPIC 9: PRACTICAL CASE STUDY – WORKFORCE PLANNING FOR A HOSPITAL
Now apply strategic HRM and workforce planning.

Situation
A hospital currently has 100 employees.

Management plans to expand its services over the next year.

The department heads provide the following workforce estimates:

Department	Current Employees	Required Next Year
Nursing	30	40
Laboratory	10	15
Biomedical	5	8
Administration	15	18
Finance	5	6
Pharmacy	10	12
Medical Support	25	30
Total	100	129
Step 1: Calculate Workforce Demand
Current Employees = 100

Future Employees Required = 129

Additional Workforce Requirement:

129 − 100 = 29 employees.

Step 2: Estimate Internal Workforce Supply
Suppose the hospital expects the following changes:

5 employees may resign.
2 employees may retire.
3 employees may transfer from other branches into the hospital.
2 existing employees may be promoted into roles that reduce the need for external recruitment.
For this simplified exercise, assume the two promoted employees remain within the hospital's total headcount and do not create additional vacancies.

Expected Workforce Supply:

100 − 5 − 2 + 3 = 96 employees.

Step 3: Calculate Workforce Gap
Future Demand = 129

Expected Supply = 96

Workforce Gap:

129 − 96 = 33 employees.

Therefore, the hospital needs to plan for 33 additional employees through recruitment or other staffing arrangements.

Step 4: Develop a Manpower Plan
Department	Additional Requirement
Nursing	10
Laboratory	5
Biomedical	3
Administration	3
Finance	1
Pharmacy	2
Medical Support	5
Total	29
The additional requirement from the department table is 29 positions.

The workforce supply calculation indicates a net gap of 33 employees.

Notice the difference: The 29 positions represent planned department-level expansion, whereas the 33-person gap also accounts for projected exits and internal transfers.

HR must reconcile these figures with department heads before finalising the recruitment plan.

This is an important real-world lesson: workforce planning requires accurate assumptions and consistent calculations.
